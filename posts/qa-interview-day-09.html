QA Interview Q&A — Day 9

Question: Over a weekend, bots pushed 38,000 stolen-card authorizations through your guest checkout. By Monday afternoon the team has added a CAPTCHA and a per-IP rate limit, and the security lead wants QA to "sign off" by 9 a.m. Tuesday. You have one afternoon. What do you test, and what does your sign-off actually say?

Context: A Hamburg-based home-and-garden shop with a custom card form that calls its payment provider's API. Most of the attack used a €1.49 seed-packet product, and about 96% of the attempts were declined. The fix has two parts: a visible CAPTCHA widget on the payment step and a CDN rule allowing five payment attempts per IP every ten minutes.

Answer

The short version

This is a security change, but it is also a performance change and an accessibility change. The fix can fail in four ways:

  1. It doesn't stop the attack. The bots get around it.
  2. It blocks or slows real customers. Shared IPs, retries after a decline and slow third-party scripts all hit genuine buyers.
  3. It shuts out disabled customers. CAPTCHA has been the top barrier in WebAIM's screen reader user surveys for years.
  4. It breaks checkout when the CAPTCHA provider is slow or down.

With one afternoon I can't test all of this deeply. So I spend the first 15 minutes asking questions that tell me where to look. Most of the remaining time goes to the "doesn't actually stop it" risk, because that is the reason for the change. Then I take quick, targeted looks at the other three. By morning I write a test report, not a permission slip. Whether to deploy is the security lead's and the product owner's call. My job is to make sure they know what they're deciding.

First 15 minutes: questions that set the test plan

I ask the security lead and the developer who built the fix:

  • Which endpoints did the bots hit? Did they go through the browser form, or call our payment endpoint directly? If they skipped the browser, a widget on the page means nothing unless the server checks the token. Stripe's card-testing guide makes the same point. If the CAPTCHA didn't stop card testing, check that every request that validates a card or creates a payment requires it, and that the check happens server-side.
  • Which endpoints are covered? Card testers like card-setup and "save card" flows as much as payments, because setup checks rarely show up on cardholders' statements. Is the CAPTCHA on create-customer and save-card too, or only on "Pay now"?
  • What is the rate limit keyed on? Behind a CDN, do we read the client IP from the right header? Can a client fake that header? And what does the limit do to many people sharing one address, such as an office network or a mobile carrier's NAT?
  • What happens if the CAPTCHA provider times out? Does checkout fail open (let people through) or fail closed (block everyone)? Who decided that, and does the product owner know it was a decision?
  • What else is in place? Are the payment provider's own fraud rules switched on? Are we refunding the fraudulent charges that did succeed, so they don't turn into disputes?

The answers often change the plan. If the bots called the API directly and the token check is client-side only, I have a high-severity finding before I've run a single test.

The afternoon, in priority order

1. Does it stop the attack? (about 2 hours, highest priority)

I test the way the attacker works, not the way a customer does: with an HTTP client, skipping the UI, against staging.

  • No token. Payment request with no CAPTCHA token. It should be rejected server-side. Repeat on save-card and create-customer.
  • Replayed token. Reuse one valid token for a second request. Cloudflare's Turnstile docs, for example, say tokens are single-use and expire after 300 seconds. Whatever vendor we use, I check that our server rejects reused and expired tokens, and doesn't just pass along whatever the widget gave it.
  • Wrong token. A token from a different site key, or a made-up string.
  • Rate limit edge cases. Rotate the forwarded-IP header. Spread requests across IPs if staging tooling allows it. Check whether the limit counts per payment attempt or per page load.
  • Responses. Compare our error messages. If a declined card gives a different message from a stolen-but-valid card that failed for another reason, we're helping the attacker sort their list.

I say plainly what IP limits can't do. Stripe's guidance is that rules built on one signal, such as IP address, usually aren't enough, and serious carders rotate through residential proxies. OWASP's OAT-001 (Carding) entry lists interaction frequency control as one countermeasure class among several, not a fix on its own. I don't need to prove the rate limit fails against a botnet. I need the decision-makers to understand it mostly raises the attacker's cost.

2. What does it do to real customers and to speed? (about 1 hour)

  • Legitimate retries. A real customer mistypes the security code (CVC) twice, then tries a second card. Do they hit the limit? What do they see: a raw 429 error, an empty page, or a message that tells them what to do?
  • Shared IPs. Five colleagues in one office ordering for a team event, or customers on a mobile carrier that puts many users behind one IP. I can't fully simulate that in an afternoon, but I can describe the scenario and ask what share of our checkout traffic comes from IPs that already show several sessions.
  • Added latency. Throttle a mid-range Android profile in DevTools and compare how long the payment step takes to become usable, with and without the widget script.
  • Blocked or slow provider. Block the CAPTCHA domain in DevTools, as an ad blocker, privacy browser or corporate proxy might, then add a long delay. Does the Pay button stay disabled forever? Does checkout fail open? Either way, I report what actually happens, not what was intended.

I'm not running a full load test. It wouldn't finish in time, and the more urgent performance question is what the change does to individual customers, not overall throughput.

3. Can disabled customers still buy? (about 1 hour)

  • Keyboard only. Tab through the full payment step. Can I reach the widget, operate it and get past it? Does focus land somewhere sensible after an error?
  • Screen readers. Use one desktop pairing (NVDA with Firefox) and VoiceOver on iOS. Is the challenge announced? Does it say what it's for? If it escalates to a visual puzzle, is there a non-visual alternative? WCAG 1.1.1 exempts CAPTCHAs from the text-alternative requirement only if their purpose is described and alternative forms in different sensory modes are provided.
  • Timing. Solve the CAPTCHA, then take six minutes to fill in the address, as a screen reader user or someone using switch access might. Submit. Does the expired token produce a clear message and a fresh challenge, or a generic failure that clears the form?
  • Zoom and reflow. At 400% zoom, does the widget overlap the Pay button?

I'd add context, without making legal claims. Since 28 June 2025 the European Accessibility Act has covered consumer e-commerce services in the EU. In Germany that happens through the BFSG, and there are exemptions such as one for microenterprises. A checkout that some customers can't complete may be more than a usability problem. Whether it's a compliance problem is a question for legal, not for me. My job is to show the barrier with evidence.

The W3C note Inaccessibility of CAPTCHA is useful in this conversation. It lists approaches that put no task on the user at all: honeypots, heuristics, rate limiting and the payment provider's own risk models. That moves the discussion from "QA is blocking a security fix" to "here are options that protect us without excluding people."

4. How will we know on Tuesday whether it's working? (30 minutes)

Before launch, I'd agree with the team on what we'll watch:

  • authorization decline rate and generic-decline errors
  • 429 counts
  • CAPTCHA pass and fail rates
  • checkout conversion by browser and device
  • support tickets that mention "robot," "can't pay" or "verification"

I'd also ask for a rollback trigger to be agreed in advance, so nobody has to argue about it mid-incident.

What not to do

  • Don't try to beat the real CAPTCHA with automation. It's built to resist that. Ask whether the vendor provides test keys or a staging bypass. Then check that the bypass can't be switched on in production. A bypass flag that leaks to production is a worse hole than the one we're closing.
  • Don't use HTSM as bureaucracy. I keep Bach's Heuristic Test Strategy Model in mind as a quick check against tunnel vision. Its quality criteria list Security, Performance, Reliability and Usability, with accessibility under Usability. Ten seconds with that list is enough to notice that a "security fix" touches all four.

What the "sign-off" says

Michael Bolton's Signing Off fits this situation: testers can sign for the quality of their testing, but the business doesn't need their permission to ship. So my message at 9 a.m. looks like this:

Testing of checkout anti-carding change: status at 08:45

What I covered: Server-side token enforcement on pay, save-card and create-customer (missing, replayed, expired and wrong-site-key tokens). Rate limit behavior including a spoofed forwarded-IP header. Keyboard and NVDA/VoiceOver walkthrough. Expired-token recovery. Behavior with the CAPTCHA domain blocked.

What I found: (1) Save-card accepts requests without a token. High severity: this is the attack's most likely path. (2) Three legitimate retries after a mistyped CVC trigger a 429 page with no explanation. (3) With the provider blocked, the Pay button stays disabled with no message (fails closed, silently). (4) The visual challenge has no audio or alternative mode in our configuration.

What I did not cover: Distributed-IP attacks at realistic scale. Real carrier-NAT traffic. iOS Safari with content blockers. Load.

What I'd want before or right after deploy: Fix (1). Agree the fail-open/closed decision explicitly. Agree a rollback trigger based on conversion and decline-rate dashboards.

The release decision is yours. I'm happy to walk through any of this.

In the meeting I'd lead with the finding that matters most to the security lead: "The fix may not stop this attack, because save-card is still open." Then the customer-impact findings, framed as tradeoffs rather than objections: "We're probably turning away some real buyers and some disabled customers. Here's roughly where, and here are options with less friction." I don't say "I can't approve this," and I don't say "looks good." Neither describes what I actually know.

Common weak answers

  • "I'd test that the CAPTCHA appears and the rate limit returns 429." That checks the fix's happy path and ignores the attacker, who never loads the page.
  • "Security is the security team's job. I'd regression-test checkout." That misses the biggest risk entirely.
  • "I'd refuse to sign off until it's WCAG compliant." That claims authority the tester doesn't have, and it frames accessibility as an obstacle to security rather than as information about who gets locked out.
  • "I'd run a load test." Wrong question for the time available. The performance risk here is to individual customers, not throughput.
  • "Sure, signed off. It's urgent." Urgency is a reason to report quickly and clearly, not a reason to imply coverage you don't have.

Follow-up questions

  1. The product owner asks you: "If the CAPTCHA provider goes down, should checkout fail open or fail closed?" How do you answer without making the decision yourself? What data would help them choose?
  2. Three weeks later, iOS Safari checkout conversion is down 4%, but support hasn't seen a spike in tickets. How would you find out whether the new controls are the cause, and what would you hold back from claiming until you know?

Sources

  • Michael Bolton (DevelopSense): Signing Off: Bolton argues testers should sign for the coverage and findings of their testing, not for the business decision to ship. The 9 a.m. report follows his structure: covered, found, not covered.
  • James Bach, Heuristic Test Strategy Model (hosted by DevelopSense): Its quality criteria (Security, Performance, Reliability, Usability with accessibility) are the quick check used here to see that one "security fix" affects several quality areas.
  • W3C APA Working Group Note: Inaccessibility of CAPTCHA: Explains why each CAPTCHA type excludes some disabled users and lists alternatives with no user task (honeypots, heuristics, rate limiting). Used to reframe the meeting around less exclusionary options.
  • W3C: Understanding WCAG Success Criterion 1.1.1 Non-text Content: The primary source for the CAPTCHA exception's conditions (purpose described, alternative sensory modes) and the extra recommendations, such as a human support path.
  • WebAIM: Screen Reader User Survey #10 Results: Shows CAPTCHA as the most problematic item reported by screen reader users. Evidence that the accessibility risk is real, not hypothetical.
  • Stripe Docs: Protect yourself from card testing: Payment-provider documentation saying the CAPTCHA must be enforced server-side on every card-validating endpoint, that card setup is a favored path, and that single-signal rules such as IP limits are usually not enough. The basis for the "does it actually stop the attack" tests.
  • Cloudflare Turnstile: Server-side validation: Vendor documentation for single-use tokens and the 300-second expiry. The basis for the replay and expired-token tests, and for the accessibility timing scenario.
  • OWASP Automated Threats: OAT-001 Carding: Defines the threat and its countermeasure classes. Supports the point that frequency control is one layer, not a complete defense.
  • Bird & Bird: A guide to navigating the European Accessibility Act for online retailers: Background on the EAA covering consumer e-commerce from 28 June 2025. Used only to explain why the barrier should go to legal, not as a legal conclusion.
